This article is part of my series Learning Cybersecurity in College: The Honest Version, where I document what it’s like to study cybersecurity as a college student — the good, the frustrating, and the things nobody warns you about.

“WTF is Wireshark?”

The message popped up in an Insta group chat we’d created with my peers from a college class called *Defending the Network*.

“Perfect timing to relax and scroll Instagram,” I thought, as the professor was explaining Wireshark features.

It might sound like I was an arrogant pro — I wasn’t. I’d just done a few things earlier that made the material feel familiar. And here they are.

Three months before my first college semester, I bought a course called ***Practical Ethical Hacking*** from TCM Security Academy. It was recommended by one of the cybersecurity professionals I talked to.

“You can get OSCP in six to nine months,” he said.

Ahaha. I couldn’t even complete the TCM Academy course. I had to quit it midway — after a few late nights of asking myself why the hell I had chosen damned cybersecurity over literally anything else.

Then came my second attempt: a Udemy course called ***The Complete Ethical Hacking Course: Beginner to Advanced!*** It covered similar topics: Nmap, reverse shells, Linux command line, Metasploit but everything was explained more gently and clearly from a beginner’s perspective. So, I stayed the course and gladly learned the ropes.

#### **College reality check**

When the first college semester finally arrived, I realized (too late) that classes had to be registered for months in advance. Of course, I hadn’t done that — what can you expect from an immigrant who had just landed in the U.S. and was still figuring out where to buy coffee that doesn’t taste like crap?

As a result, for Spring 2025 all that was left for me were only irrelevant general classes required to satisfy the college’s program. Frustrated, I decided to entertain myself in parallel with ***TryHackMe*** and ***Hack The Box***.

That turned out to be my best bet.

Udemy and TryHackMe gave me the context behind the theory, so by the time I reached *Defending the Network*, the class felt easy — I ended up with an A without much effort. Without it, I would have felt just as overwhelmed as many of my classmates, suddenly pummeled with dozens of new tech concepts and heavy acronyms.

Motivated by this success, during my fall semester — while taking Microcomputer Essentials — I decided to embark on another side journey. I dreamt about eJPT and purchased ***INE’s*** course materials, proving once again that English teachers do more than just correct spelling and grammar and drink coffee.

#### Pace & motivation: fun vs. formal education

In college, I learned pretty quickly that motivation doesn’t disappear — it just hibernates when learning starts to feel like punishment. That became obvious when I compared my formal education with platforms like TryHackMe.

On TryHackMe, learning feels like a game without pressure — you move forward when you’re ready, not when the syllabus or the professor says so. Gamification through points, badges, and progress bars turns abstract concepts into small, tangible wins. Mistakes are part of the process, and community events — like *Advent of Cyber* — add motivation without the stress of grades. Overall, it feels like progress — not detention.

That said, these platforms aren’t perfect. Learning paths can feel scattered, with topics that don’t always build logically upon each other. Some steps are skipped entirely, and you’re occasionally expected to “just know” something — with no instructor to ask. Explanations can be inconsistent: some rooms go into extreme detail, while others assume a level of prior knowledge you may not have yet. And despite being labeled beginner-friendly, many THM and HTB “beginner” challenges are anything but. When I got stuck, I often started questioning my own ability rather than the structure of the material.

College had its own challenges. Early on, courses built around TestOut materials and PowerPoint-heavy lectures gave me anxiety. Concepts were often presented in isolation and assessed through multiple-choice questions that rewarded memorization rather than understanding. The problem wasn’t the difficulty; it was the format.

That’s when the downside of live lectures became obvious. Committing an entire evening to a three-hour real-time explanation meant committing to a single pace and a single chance to “get it.” If you missed a point the lecture moved on without you — unfortunately, modern education still doesn’t support a “watch again” feature. There’s no option to play it at 2× speed either, when the concept is familiar or the professor’s pace is simply too slow for you.

And yet, despite all of this, college helps a lot — just in a different way.

#### What college still does well: people

The best part of college turned out to be networking — not the TCP/IP kind, but the human one. The professors I studied with were genuinely willing to dedicate their time: answering questions, explaining concepts again when needed, and sometimes even jumping on a phone call to talk through my future career or certification plans.

College is also full of like-minded students. You meet people dealing with the same struggles, thinking about similar career paths, or simply trying to crack professors’ instructions and grading criteria. A couple of peers I met along the way were already working in IT and were happy to share practical insights about job requirements — and even offered to put in a good word for me if an opening ever came up.

Another advantage of college is access to opportunities that are much harder to reach on your own. Internships, career fairs, and employer info sessions bring companies and recruiters directly to campus. Headhunters actively come looking for talent, not just polished CVs, and being a good student often provides an edge.

For someone who finds LinkedIn networking cringy, this type of low-pressure connection with people felt like a huge relief.
